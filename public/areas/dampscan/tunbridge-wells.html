Damp survey in Tunbridge Wells

Tunbridge Wells is built on sandstone on a hill, in a mixture of Regency stucco around the Pantiles and Calverley, high Victorian brick villas, and the tile hanging and weatherboarding that the Wealden tradition brought with it. The slope and the tile hanging between them account for most of what we are called to, along with the basements and lower ground floors that the hillside made inevitable.

Tunbridge Wells homes, and how they get damp

Tile hung elevations are everywhere here, and they fail quietly. The tiles shed water off the face while the battens and felt behind them do the real work, and neither is visible. A handful of slipped or frost damaged tiles lets water onto battens that rot, and the first indication inside is a damp patch on an upper floor wall with a perfectly respectable looking elevation outside. It has to be inspected close up, rather than from the garden.

The Regency and early Victorian stucco fronted terraces have the same issue as their London counterparts: a render skin over solid brick, which holds water against the wall once it cracks or blows, and which gets made worse by impermeable modern paint. On a sloping site the lower ground floors of these terraces also sit into the hill, so the rear or uphill wall is retaining ground with no drainage behind it.

The sandstone the town sits on drains freely, which is usually helpful, but it also means surface water moves fast on a slope. Gardens above a house shed water at it during heavy rain, and a blocked or absent drainage channel at the top of a plot puts that water straight against the wall. This produces damp that is sharply seasonal and correlates with heavy rainfall rather than being constant, which is a useful thing to be able to demonstrate.

Ground, water and weather in Tunbridge Wells

The town stands on the Tunbridge Wells Sand, part of the High Weald, with outcrops of sandstone at High Rocks, Toad Rock in Rusthall and the Common. Between the sandstone ridges are bands of clay, and valleys like the Grom and the streams towards High Brooms collect water. The chalybeate spring that gave the town its name is a reminder that groundwater is never far away on the lower ground.

Rising damp, penetrating damp or condensation?

They can look the same on a Tunbridge Wells wall: a stain, a tide mark, blown plaster or mould. They behave differently. Rising damp is steady through the year and highest at the base of a wall. Penetrating damp follows the weather and sits where water has a route in, around a window, under a gutter, on the exposed side. Condensation is worst in cold weather, on the coldest surfaces, and often comes with streaming windows.

A meter alone cannot tell them apart, and salts in old plaster make every damp meter read high. The pattern, the readings at depth, the outside of the building and the room conditions together can, which is why the survey takes the time it does.

Inside and out: the Tunbridge Wells survey, step by step

Most damp is decided outside the house, so that is where half the visit goes. On a typical Tunbridge Wells property we check:

  • The outside walls first: pointing, render, sills, and how sandstone, stucco and tile hanging has been repaired over the years
  • Where the ground sits against the wall, front and back, and whether a patio or flower bed has crept up
  • Rainwater: gutters, hoppers, downpipes and where they discharge, ideally after we have seen them in rain
  • Damp readings inside on a pattern, with a reference reading from a dry wall for comparison
  • Salts, tide marks and blown plaster, which say what the water has been doing for years
  • Room conditions: humidity, dew point and the coldest surfaces, for any mould
  • Timber in reach: skirtings, joist ends, sub floor and the loft hatch view of the roof

A sample report, in outline

No two reports are the same, but a Tunbridge Wells report usually runs in this order:

  • Property and conditions on the day, including recent weather
  • Findings by room and by elevation, each with photographs
  • Readings and the pattern they make, not a single headline figure
  • Diagnosis: the cause, and the alternatives that were tested and excluded
  • Recommendations as a specification, with the order to do them in
  • Anything not inspected, and exactly where to look if it needs opening up

Damp survey questions from Tunbridge Wells

How do you inspect tile hanging without taking it apart?

Close up visual inspection, combined with internal moisture readings that establish where the water is arriving and in what pattern. Slipped, cracked and frost damaged tiles are visible once you are at the elevation, and the internal pattern tells you which part to concentrate on. Where the battens behind are in question, the report specifies where a small area should be lifted.

The damp is worse after heavy rain and dries out between. What causes that?

That pattern almost always points at water arriving from outside rather than rising from below. On a sloping site here the usual causes are garden run off reaching the wall, an overflowing gutter or hopper, a failed tile hung elevation on the weather side, or blown render. Rising damp does not switch on and off with the weather.

Our lower ground floor flat has a damp rear wall. Is that rising damp?

Usually it is lateral water from the hillside behind, because the rear wall is retaining ground. That needs drainage and, where appropriate, a below ground system, not injection.

Do you cover Southborough, Pembury and the villages?

Yes, and Rusthall, Langton Green, Speldhurst, Bidborough and the rest of the borough.

Our freeholder says the damp is our problem. Is it?

If the water comes through the structure, the render or the roof, it is often the freeholder's responsibility under the lease. The report identifies the source, which is the evidence you need.

How soon can you come?

Same day reply and a visit usually within a few days. Tunbridge Wells is about half an hour from our base.

Will you try to sell me damp proofing in Tunbridge Wells?

No. DampScan is a survey and report practice and carries out no remedial work of any kind. Where work is needed, the report specifies it so any contractor can price it, and where it is not, the report says so.
