Damp survey in Swanley

Swanley grew quickly in the second half of the twentieth century around a railway junction, and most of its houses date from the 1950s onwards. That makes it unlike the old market towns around it: here, damp is less about lime and ragstone and much more about cavity walls, concrete floors, replacement windows and the way families live in houses that were built tighter than the generation before.

Swanley homes, and how they get damp

The large post war estates across the town are mostly cavity brick and block, often with concrete ground floors and timber or steel framed windows long since replaced in uPVC. Their weak points are well known: concrete lintels and slab edges that stay cold, cavity wall insulation added later that can bridge or slump, and bathrooms and kitchens with extract fans that move far less air than they should.

There is older building as well. Swanley Village and the lanes around Crockenhill and Hextable have Victorian cottages and farmhouses, some with flint or brick walls and no original damp proof course. The Darent valley villages close by, Eynsford and Farningham, have medieval and Georgian buildings, timber frames and flint, where breathable repairs matter far more than modern treatments.

Many Swanley houses have been extended at the back or the side. Where a new extension meets an old wall, the damp proof course and cavity tray have to be lapped properly, and where the floor of the extension has been laid higher than the original course, the old wall becomes damp at its base. That junction is one of the commonest causes we find in the town.

Ground, water and weather in Swanley

The town sits on the chalk dip slope above the Darent valley, with clay with flints and patches of sand and gravel on the higher ground. Water drains steadily on the chalk but lingers on the clay caps, so gardens on the higher estates can stay wet through winter and shed water towards houses on sloping plots. The Darent itself is a chalk stream to the east, and the valley floor at Eynsford and Farningham sits close to the water table.

What we find most often in Swanley

  • Condensation and black mould on cold lintels, reveals and slab edges in post war houses
  • Extension floors laid above the original damp proof course, wetting the old wall at the junction
  • Cavity wall insulation bridging moisture across to the inner leaf on exposed elevations
  • Bathroom and kitchen extract fans undersized or ducted into the loft
  • No damp proof course at all in older cottages in Swanley Village and Crockenhill

Rising damp, penetrating damp or condensation?

They can look the same on a Swanley wall: a stain, a tide mark, blown plaster or mould. They behave differently. Rising damp is steady through the year and highest at the base of a wall. Penetrating damp follows the weather and sits where water has a route in, around a window, under a gutter, on the exposed side. Condensation is worst in cold weather, on the coldest surfaces, and often comes with streaming windows.

A meter alone cannot tell them apart, and salts in old plaster make every damp meter read high. The pattern, the readings at depth, the outside of the building and the room conditions together can, which is why the survey takes the time it does.

What we check on a damp survey in Swanley

The visit follows the water. Whatever brought you to us, in Swanley we work through:

  • The roofline and chimney from the ground and from windows, then gutters and downpipes in detail
  • Every outside wall for cracks, open joints, failed render and anything bridging the base of the wall
  • Airbricks and sub floor ventilation, often buried by later paths and extensions
  • Internal walls with calibrated readings at height bands, so rising, penetrating and condensation damp can be told apart
  • Surface and air conditions in the problem rooms, logged rather than estimated
  • Kitchen and bathroom extraction, measured at the grille
  • Any earlier damp proofing, its paperwork and whether it was ever the right answer

Damp survey questions from Swanley

Our 1960s house has black mould every winter. Is that condensation?

Probably, but condensation is a symptom with a cause. Cold surfaces at lintels and slab edges, cavity insulation problems and failed extraction all make it worse, and those are building matters. Surface temperature and humidity readings show which, so the report can say what will actually stop it.

The wall where our extension joins the house is damp. Why?

Often the extension floor was laid higher than the original damp proof course, or the cavity tray at the junction was not lapped properly. Both let water into the old wall at its base. It is a detail fault, not rising damp, and the survey finds where.

Should we have our cavity insulation removed?

Only if it is shown to be causing a problem. Damp on an exposed elevation that follows driving rain, with readings highest where the insulation is, can point to bridging. We check rather than assume, because removal is expensive and often unnecessary.

Do you cover Hextable, Eynsford and West Kingsdown?

Yes, and Farningham, Horton Kirby, South Darenth and the villages around Swanley. The fee is the same.

Is a positive input ventilation unit worth fitting?

Sometimes. It suits houses with a loft and a fairly open layout. It does not fix cold bridges, and in some layouts a properly working extract fan does more. The report recommends what the measurements support, and we sell neither.

Same day reply, and a visit usually within a few days, including evenings and weekends.

Will you try to sell me damp proofing in Swanley?

No. DampScan is a survey and report practice and carries out no remedial work of any kind. Where work is needed, the report specifies it so any contractor can price it, and where it is not, the report says so.
