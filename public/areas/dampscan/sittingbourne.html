Damp survey in Sittingbourne

Sittingbourne made bricks. The brickearth around the town and along Milton Creek fed the yellow stock brick that built much of Victorian London, and the town's own terraces are built of it. Add the paper mills at Kemsley, the older town of Milton Regis with its timber framed core, and a large amount of housing from the last forty years at Iwade, Kemsley and the edges of the town, and you have a place where damp comes in several distinct forms.

Sittingbourne homes, and how they get damp

The Victorian and Edwardian terraces around the town centre, Murston and towards Milton are solid stock brick in lime mortar, with slate roofs and rear additions. Stock brick is soft and absorbent, and copes well with wetting and drying when the mortar and plaster are lime. When it is repointed in hard cement mortar, or plastered inside with gypsum or sand and cement, the moisture it takes in has nowhere to go, and the faces of the bricks start to spall outside while the plaster blows inside.

Milton Regis has some of the oldest buildings in Swale, timber framed houses around the High Street and the Court Hall, and weatherboarded buildings near the creek. These sit on lower, wetter ground and need lime and breathable repairs, not modern treatments.

Much of the town's growth since the 1980s, at Kemsley, Iwade, Grovehurst and the estates to the south and west, is cavity walled with modern courses. On those homes, damp is overwhelmingly condensation, ventilation, cavity tray faults at extensions and conservatories, and drives or patios laid too high.

Ground, water and weather in Sittingbourne

Sittingbourne lies on brickearth and London Clay sloping gently down to the Swale, with Milton Creek cutting into the town from the north. The creek side and the marsh edges are low and wet, with a high water table and tidal influence. Clay drains slowly, so gardens stay wet in winter and water lingers at the base of walls. To the south, the land rises onto the chalk of the Downs at Borden and Tunstall, where drainage is much better.

The brickfields and the town they built

For a century and more, the land around Sittingbourne and Milton Creek was dug for brickearth and fired into yellow stock bricks, much of which went by barge to London. The town itself is built of the same brick, which is why its Victorian terraces look so much like parts of south and east London.

That shared construction means shared problems: soft brick that needs lime mortar, rear additions with lean to roofs, yards that have been raised, and suspended timber floors that need their airbricks open. It also means shared mistakes, and the commonest is treating the wall with injection and dense plaster when the cause is outside.

Three kinds of damp, three different fixes

Rising damp needs the ground moisture stopped at the base of the wall. Penetrating damp needs the route in closed: a gutter, a joint, a sill, a flashing. Condensation needs less moisture in the air, more air movement and warmer surfaces. In Sittingbourne, as everywhere, these get confused constantly, and a remedy for one does nothing for the others.

That is the case for an independent survey in a sentence. Somebody needs to work out which problem you have before anybody sells you the fix for one of them.

A survey here is a morning or an afternoon with one qualified surveyor, not a salesman with a meter. In Sittingbourne that means:

Buying or letting in Sittingbourne

Sittingbourne is a popular place to buy, with fast trains to London and a wide range of homes. Damp survey questions from Sittingbourne

Why are the bricks on our house crumbling?

Soft stock bricks repointed in hard cement mortar can no longer lose moisture through the joints, so it leaves through the brick face, taking the surface with it in frost. Repointing in lime, after raking out the cement carefully, stops it getting worse.

Is rising damp common in Sittingbourne?

It is diagnosed far more often than it occurs. On the terraces, the cause is usually raised ground, cement plaster or salts. On newer homes, it is usually condensation or a construction detail. The survey separates them.

We live near Milton Creek and the floor is damp. Why?

The water table near the creek is high and responds to tide and rain. Floors there may be affected by groundwater, which needs a different approach from a wall treatment.

Do you cover Iwade, Borden and Bapchild?

Yes, and Kemsley, Tunstall, Bobbing, Newington and the rest of the area around Sittingbourne.

Our conservatory wall is damp. What is the cause?

Often the conservatory floor or base was built above the house's damp proof course, or there is no cavity tray where the roof meets the wall. Both are detailing faults the survey can identify.
