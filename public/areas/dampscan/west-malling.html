Damp survey in West Malling

West Malling is a small market town with a long, broad High Street of Georgian frontages over older buildings, a Norman tower, an abbey still in use and a conservation area covering most of the centre. It is one of the best preserved small towns in Kent, which means the right damp advice here is almost always about keeping old buildings working the way they were built, rather than adding modern systems to them.

West Malling homes, and how they get damp

Many High Street buildings are older than their Georgian fronts suggest. A brick or rendered face can hide a timber frame, or a ragstone wall, behind it. That matters for damp, because a brick skin added to a timber frame, or a cement render over stone, changes how the wall handles moisture, and the decay that results is often in a part of the structure nobody can see without opening up.

Behind the High Street and along Swan Street and West Street there are smaller cottages in ragstone and brick, some with tile hung upper storeys, and Victorian houses towards the station. These have suspended timber floors, lime plaster and, in many cases, no original damp proof course. Garden levels have risen against them for a century or more, and their gutters and valleys are old.

Around the edges of the town and in Leybourne, the housing is twentieth century and later, including the developments at Leybourne Grange and Leybourne Chase, built in the last couple of decades. There the questions are new build ones: ventilation, construction moisture, and the details at thresholds, balconies and roof junctions.

Ground, water and weather in West Malling

West Malling sits on the Hythe Beds ragstone with sands and clays around it, on rising ground below the North Downs. Streams run from the springs at the foot of the Downs through the town towards the Medway, and the abbey grounds have their own watercourse and lake. Old walls close to these streams sit in ground that stays wetter, and the steep lanes towards Offham shed water quickly in heavy rain.

Rising damp, penetrating damp or condensation?

They can look the same on a West Malling wall: a stain, a tide mark, blown plaster or mould. They behave differently. Rising damp is steady through the year and highest at the base of a wall. Penetrating damp follows the weather and sits where water has a route in, around a window, under a gutter, on the exposed side. Condensation is worst in cold weather, on the coldest surfaces, and often comes with streaming windows.

A meter alone cannot tell them apart, and salts in old plaster make every damp meter read high. The pattern, the readings at depth, the outside of the building and the room conditions together can, which is why the survey takes the time it does.

What a DampScan survey in West Malling covers

A survey here is a morning or an afternoon with one qualified surveyor, not a salesman with a meter. In West Malling that means:

  • Moisture readings across a grid on every affected wall, not just the stain, so the shape of the damp is on record
  • A look at Kentish ragstone and Georgian brick or brick outside, joint by joint where it matters, because the cause is usually on the outside face
  • Ground and path levels against each elevation, measured against the damp proof course or the internal floor
  • Gutters, downpipes, gullies and any drain covers we can lift
  • Air temperature, humidity and surface temperatures, so condensation is measured rather than guessed
  • Ventilation: whether fans extract to outside and how much air they actually move
  • Floor voids and exposed timbers where they can be reached, for rot and beetle

A sample report, in outline

No two reports are the same, but a West Malling report usually runs in this order:

  • Property and conditions on the day, including recent weather
  • Findings by room and by elevation, each with photographs
  • Readings and the pattern they make, not a single headline figure
  • Diagnosis: the cause, and the alternatives that were tested and excluded
  • Recommendations as a specification, with the order to do them in
  • Anything not inspected, and exactly where to look if it needs opening up

Damp survey questions from West Malling

Our Georgian front is rendered and the inside wall is damp. What is happening?

Render that has cracked, or been replaced in cement and sealed with modern paint, holds water against the wall and stops it drying outwards. The wall then dries inwards and shows damp. The survey checks the render closely and what is behind it, where that can be seen.

Can you survey a listed building without damaging it?

Yes. We inspect, measure and photograph, and only recommend opening up where it would change the conclusion. Any opening is specified precisely so consent can be sought for that and nothing more.

We have woodworm in the roof. Does it need treating?

Not necessarily. Much woodworm in old roofs is historic. Active beetle needs damp timber, so we check moisture content and for fresh signs before saying whether treatment is justified.

Our Leybourne new build has mould. Who is responsible?

If the cause is a ventilation system that does not perform or a construction defect, it may be the developer within the warranty period. Our report measures and records the cause in a form a developer or warranty provider can act on.

Will you try to sell me damp proofing in West Malling?

No. DampScan is a survey and report practice and carries out no remedial work of any kind. Where work is needed, the report specifies it so any contractor can price it, and where it is not, the report says so.
