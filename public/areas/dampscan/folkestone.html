Damp survey in Folkestone

Folkestone has two faces. On the West End and along the Leas, grand Victorian and Edwardian terraces and villas built for a fashionable resort, most now divided into flats. Around the harbour and the Old High Street, older, smaller buildings and a revived creative quarter. Behind them, Cheriton, Morehall and Hawkinge are mostly twentieth century. The town faces south over the Channel, which brings wind driven rain and salt.

Folkestone homes, and how they get damp

The Leas and the West End are lined with tall stucco and brick buildings, many with basements, bays, balconies and complex roofs with parapets and valleys. Converted into flats, they have shared roofs, gutters and drains, and damp in a top floor flat is often a parapet or valley problem in a common part. Basement flats often retain ground at the rear.

Around the harbour and the Old High Street, the buildings are older and smaller, in brick, render and sometimes local stone, on steep ground. Rear walls retain the slope, and the steep streets send water downhill quickly in heavy rain.

Cheriton, Morehall and Hawkinge are mostly interwar, post war and newer. Mould, condensation and ventilation are the main issues, with flat roofed extensions and bridged courses.

Ground, water and weather in Folkestone

Folkestone sits where the chalk of the North Downs meets the Lower Greensand and the Gault clay. The Warren to the east is famous for landslips, and Sandgate to the west sits on ground known for slow movement. Clay holds water and moves with the seasons, while greensand drains freely. The mix makes local drainage and ground conditions especially relevant to damp.

Big houses, small flats

Folkestone's grand houses were built with large roofs, many chimneys, parapets hiding valley gutters and long runs of cast iron rainwater pipes. Divided into flats, these are now nobody's in particular, and maintenance can slip for years. Water from a blocked valley can travel a long way inside a wall before it shows, often in a flat two floors down, which is why the source and the symptom are frequently in different flats.

Chimneys are another recurring source. Stacks that have been capped without ventilation trap moisture in the flue, which condenses and soaks through the breast, leaving brown stains on chimney breasts in flats far below. The fix is at the top, not in the room.

At the other end of the building, many basements were once kitchens and service rooms, now converted into flats. Their rear walls hold back the ground and their front walls face an area or lightwell. Damp in them is lateral or from blocked area drains far more often than it is rising damp.

Telling the kinds of damp apart

Most of the cost of a damp problem comes from treating the wrong one. Water rising from the ground, rain getting in from outside and moisture condensing from the air inside all leave marks on a Folkestone wall, and the marks overlap.

The differences are in when and where. Damp that comes and goes with rain points outside. Damp that is worst in a cold snap, behind furniture and in corners, points to condensation. Damp that is constant, rises to a fairly even height and falls off above it, on a wall with no outside fault, is the one that might be rising. The survey records which of these the evidence supports.

What a DampScan survey in Folkestone covers

A survey here is a morning or an afternoon with one qualified surveyor, not a salesman with a meter. In Folkestone that means:

  • Moisture readings across a grid on every affected wall, not just the stain, so the shape of the damp is on record
  • A look at Victorian brick, stucco and stone or brick outside, joint by joint where it matters, because the cause is usually on the outside face
  • Ground and path levels against each elevation, measured against the damp proof course or the internal floor
  • Gutters, downpipes, gullies and any drain covers we can lift
  • Air temperature, humidity and surface temperatures, so condensation is measured rather than guessed
  • Ventilation: whether fans extract to outside and how much air they actually move
  • Floor voids and exposed timbers where they can be reached, for rot and beetle

Buying or letting in Folkestone

Many Folkestone buyers are buying a flat in a converted Leas or West End house. Damp in those flats often comes from the building, not the flat, so knowing the source and responsibility before exchange is essential. An independent survey gives you that, and gives you evidence for the freeholder if work is needed.

Letting in Folkestone? Damp and mould in rented homes are a hazard under the Housing Health and Safety Rating System, and social landlords in England now have fixed timescales to investigate and act under Awaab's Law, with the government planning to extend similar duties to private renting. An independent report that separates building defects from condensation is the evidence both landlords and tenants need. See our guide to landlord damp and mould duties.

Damp survey questions from Folkestone

Our top floor flat on the Leas has damp ceilings. Who fixes it?

If the source is the roof, a parapet or a valley gutter, it is usually a common part and the freeholder's responsibility. Our report identifies the source.

Our basement flat has a damp rear wall. Why?

It is usually retaining ground at the back, with water arriving sideways. That needs drainage and a below ground system, not injection.

Does the ground in Sandgate affect damp?

Ground movement can crack drains and walls, which can let water in. The survey looks for signs of that alongside other causes.

Do you cover Hythe, Hawkinge and Capel le Ferne?

Yes, and the rest of the Folkestone and Hythe district.

Our Cheriton house has mould every winter. Is that a building issue?

Often partly. Cold spots, failed extraction and new windows without trickle vents are common. Measurements show which.

How soon can you visit?

Same day reply and usually within a few days.

Will you try to sell me damp proofing in Folkestone?

No. DampScan is a survey and report practice and carries out no remedial work of any kind. Where work is needed, the report specifies it so any contractor can price it, and where it is not, the report says so.
