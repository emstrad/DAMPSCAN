Damp survey in Snodland

Snodland grew in the nineteenth century around paper making and cement, on the west bank of the Medway, and much of the town is still made up of the Victorian terraces built for those works. Since then it has added post war housing and, more recently, large new developments such as Holborough Lakes on the site of a former cement works. Old brick by the river and new building on reworked ground make a distinctive mix.

Snodland homes, and how they get damp

The Victorian and Edwardian terraces along the High Street and the streets running towards the river are solid brick, many built quickly for workers, with shallow foundations, small rear yards and suspended or solid floors. Their damp is usually at the back: yards concreted over, outside toilets turned into utility rooms, and downpipes discharging at the base of the wall.

Post war and later twentieth century housing fills much of the town to the west of the railway and towards Ham Hill. Cavity walls, concrete floors and replacement windows mean condensation and mould are the most common issues, along with drives laid over the damp proof course.

Holborough Lakes and other newer developments sit on former industrial ground with lakes where quarries were. New build questions dominate: whether ventilation is working, whether construction moisture is still drying out, and whether details at balconies and thresholds were done properly.

Ground, water and weather in Snodland

Snodland sits between the river and the chalk of the North Downs. The low ground near the Medway is alluvium and gravels with a high water table, while the land rises to chalk towards Holborough and Paddlesworth, where quarrying for cement left deep pits now flooded as lakes. Streets close to the river are low lying and the river is tidal up to Allington, so groundwater responds to both rain and tide.

Industry, river and reworked ground

Snodland's growth followed its industries: the paper mill by the river and the cement and lime works on the chalk towards Holborough. The terraces were built close to the works and to the river, often quickly, and many have shallow foundations and small yards. Over a century and more, yards have been raised and concreted, outbuildings joined to houses, and drains extended and patched.

Holborough Lakes and other newer developments are built on land that was quarried and worked, now restored around flooded pits. Drainage on such sites is engineered, and new homes there are built to modern standards, so damp is most often about ventilation and construction details rather than ground. The survey looks at each house on its own terms.

Three kinds of damp, three different fixes

Rising damp needs the ground moisture stopped at the base of the wall. Penetrating damp needs the route in closed: a gutter, a joint, a sill, a flashing. Condensation needs less moisture in the air, more air movement and warmer surfaces. In Snodland, as everywhere, these get confused constantly, and a remedy for one does nothing for the others.

That is the case for an independent survey in a sentence. Somebody needs to work out which problem you have before anybody sells you the fix for one of them.

What we check on a damp survey in Snodland

The visit follows the water. Whatever brought you to us, in Snodland we work through:

  • The roofline and chimney from the ground and from windows, then gutters and downpipes in detail
  • Every outside wall for cracks, open joints, failed render and anything bridging the base of the wall
  • Airbricks and sub floor ventilation, often buried by later paths and extensions
  • Internal walls with calibrated readings at height bands, so rising, penetrating and condensation damp can be told apart
  • Surface and air conditions in the problem rooms, logged rather than estimated
  • Kitchen and bathroom extraction, measured at the grille
  • Any earlier damp proofing, its paperwork and whether it was ever the right answer

A sample report, in outline

No two reports are the same, but a Snodland report usually runs in this order:

  • Property and conditions on the day, including recent weather
  • Findings by room and by elevation, each with photographs
  • Readings and the pattern they make, not a single headline figure
  • Diagnosis: the cause, and the alternatives that were tested and excluded
  • Recommendations as a specification, with the order to do them in
  • Anything not inspected, and exactly where to look if it needs opening up

Letting in Snodland? Damp and mould in rented homes are a hazard under the Housing Health and Safety Rating System, and social landlords in England now have fixed timescales to investigate and act under Awaab's Law, with the government planning to extend similar duties to private renting. Damp survey questions from Snodland

Our Victorian terrace has damp in the back room. Is that rising damp?

Usually it is the yard, a downpipe or a former outside toilet built against the wall. The survey looks at the outside first and the readings show the pattern. Injection would not deal with any of those causes.

We are close to the river. Could the damp be groundwater?

On the lowest streets it can be, especially in floors. Groundwater damp tends to vary with season and is spread across floors rather than following a line up a wall. The survey separates groundwater from other causes.

Our new house at Holborough Lakes has mould. Is that a defect?

If ventilation is not working as designed, or water is getting in at a detail, it can be. We measure ventilation, humidity and surface temperatures and write the report so it can go to the developer or warranty provider.

Do you cover Halling and Upper Halling?

Yes, and Paddlesworth, Birling and the rest of the area between Snodland and Rochester.

Our 1970s house has streaming windows every morning. Is that normal?

Some condensation on windows in cold weather is normal. Heavy, daily condensation with mould on walls suggests too much moisture and too little ventilation, and often cold spots. The report sets out what will make the biggest difference.

No. DampScan is a survey and report practice and carries out no remedial work of any kind. Where work is needed, the report specifies it so any contractor can price it, and where it is not, the report says so.
