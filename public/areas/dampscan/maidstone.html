Damp survey in Maidstone

Maidstone sits in the Medway valley on Kentish ragstone, and both facts show up in the surveys. Ragstone is a beautiful, hard wearing walling stone and a poor barrier to water when the mortar around it fails, and a valley floor town has a high water table in the low lying parts and steep run off in the high ones. Add a large stock of Victorian terraces and a ring of post war estates, and Maidstone produces almost every kind of damp there is.

Maidstone homes, and how they get damp

Ragstone walls, common in the older parts of the town and the surrounding villages, are typically rubble filled: two faces of stone with a core of smaller stone and lime mortar between them. They work by staying breathable. Repointed in cement, as many have been, the wall can still take water in through the joints and the face but can no longer let it out, and it moves inwards instead. Sizeable sums get spent on internal treatments here for what is a pointing problem.

The Victorian terraces around the town centre, up the Tonbridge Road and towards Tovil are solid brick with slate roofs and rear closet wings, and the usual pattern is external ground level. Successive resurfacing of yards and passageways has raised the ground against these walls well above the original damp proof course. The town is also low lying along the river, so where the water table is high the two problems compound and are easily mistaken for one.

Out towards Bearsted, Boxley and the villages to the south, the stock changes again to timber frame, weatherboarding and tile hanging, often listed or in a conservation area. Timber framed buildings need to breathe in a way that modern impermeable repairs prevent. Cement render or gypsum plaster on a timber frame traps moisture directly against the frame, and the decay that follows is structural rather than cosmetic.

Ground, water and weather in Maidstone

The Medway runs through the middle of the town, joined by the Len from the east and the Loose stream from the south, and the ground falls steeply to all three. Riverside streets and anything built on the old wharves sit low, with cellars and solid floors close to the water table. The higher ground at Penenden Heath, Shepway and Park Wood drains quickly, which moves the problem to surface water running at the uphill side of a house. The ragstone beds that gave the town its building stone lie close under much of the south side, and clays appear on the lower slopes.

What we find most often in Maidstone

  • Ragstone walls repointed in cement, taking water in and no longer able to release it
  • Raised yards and passageways bridging the damp proof course on Victorian terraces near the centre
  • High water table affecting cellars and solid floors in the low lying parts of the valley
  • Cement render and gypsum plaster trapping moisture against a timber frame in period village property
  • Condensation and mould in 1960s and 1970s houses and flats on the estates, often after window replacement

Around Maidstone

Town centre and the riverside

Georgian and Victorian buildings over shops, converted warehouses and flats close to the Medway. Cellars are common, and so are flats above shops where a defective parapet gutter feeds water down a party wall two floors before it shows.

Bearsted and Thurnham

Older village cottages around the green, larger inter war and post war houses beyond. The period houses have solid walls and suspended timber floors, and the most common faults are blocked airbricks and garden beds built up against the walls.

Loose, Coxheath and the south

Ragstone cottages along the Loose valley, many listed, and twentieth century housing up on the ridge. Down in the valley the stream and the springs keep the ground wet, and lime based repairs matter far more than any injection.

Telling the kinds of damp apart

Most of the cost of a damp problem comes from treating the wrong one. Water rising from the ground, rain getting in from outside and moisture condensing from the air inside all leave marks on a Maidstone wall, and the marks overlap.

The differences are in when and where. Damp that comes and goes with rain points outside. Damp that is worst in a cold snap, behind furniture and in corners, points to condensation. Damp that is constant, rises to a fairly even height and falls off above it, on a wall with no outside fault, is the one that might be rising. The survey records which of these the evidence supports.

Buying or letting in Maidstone

A Maidstone mortgage valuation or homebuyer report will often note "damp readings" on a ragstone or solid brick wall and recommend a specialist. That specialist is usually a treatment firm offering a free survey. An independent survey before exchange tells you whether the readings mean anything, what the real cause is, and roughly what fixing it involves, which is what you need to negotiate on price or walk away.

Letting in Maidstone? Damp and mould in rented homes are a hazard under the Housing Health and Safety Rating System, and social landlords in England now have fixed timescales to investigate and act under Awaab's Law, with the government planning to extend similar duties to private renting. Damp survey questions from Maidstone

Our ragstone wall is damp inside. Does it need tanking?

Very rarely, and tanking a solid stone wall usually moves the problem rather than solving it. A rubble filled ragstone wall is designed to take some water and release it again through breathable lime pointing and plaster. If it has been repointed in cement, or rendered externally, the release path is closed and the moisture comes out on the inside face. The remedy is normally to reopen that path with the right mortar and internal finish.

We are near the river and the cellar is damp. Is that just the water table?

It might be, and it is worth proving rather than assuming, because the two look identical from inside. A high water table produces damp that varies with rainfall and river level and is fairly even across the below ground structure. A defect, such as a cracked drain, a bridged wall or surface water running at the building, is more localised and often has a clear direction. We survey both before saying which it is.

Our 1970s house in Park Wood has mould every winter. Is that a building fault?

Often it is at least partly. Houses of that era tend to have thin cavity walls, cold concrete lintels and windows that were replaced without trickle vents. Mould in a neat band over a window or along the ceiling edge is following a cold bridge, and that is a building matter rather than how you live. We measure the surface temperatures so the report can say which.

Can you look at a listed cottage without causing damage?

Yes. Our survey is non invasive by default: readings, inspection and photographs. If opening up would genuinely change the conclusion, we say where and why, and nothing is disturbed without your agreement. For listed buildings the report also notes where consent would be needed for any recommended work.
