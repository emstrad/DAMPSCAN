Damp survey in Dartford

Dartford sits on chalk where the Darent meets the Thames, and the two halves of that sentence explain most of what we find. Chalk drains fast, so damp arrives and departs with the weather. The riverside and marsh edge at Stone and Greenhithe is low, alluvial and altogether wetter. Between them is a town of Victorian terraces, interwar semis and a great deal of new building, each with its own way of getting damp.

Dartford homes, and how they get damp

On the chalk, water moves through the ground quickly rather than accumulating, which gives damp a distinctly episodic character: it appears during sustained rain and eases within days. That pattern points at surface water reaching the building, at ground falls and blocked gullies, rather than at anything continuous. It is one of the most useful diagnostic signals available in this part of Kent and it is regularly overlooked in favour of a treatment.

The town has a good deal of Victorian and early twentieth century terrace around the centre and up West Hill, solid walled with rear yards. Successive resurfacing of those yards has raised ground above the damp proof course on many of them. There is also a large amount of interwar and post war semi across Wilmington, Joydens Wood and Temple Hill, cavity walled with courses that generally still work, where the same bridging fault dominates.

The riverside at Stone, Greenhithe and Swanscombe is former industrial and quarry land, much of it redeveloped, and Ebbsfleet and Ingress Park have added thousands of newer homes. Made ground behaves unpredictably, drainage is often relatively new but laid in variable material, and the water table near the marsh is high. Solid floors and any below ground space there need assessing on their own terms rather than by the standards of the chalk uphill.

Ground, water and weather in Dartford

The Darent runs north through the town to the Thames, and the land falls from the chalk of the downs to the river flats and the Dartford and Stone marshes. Chalk quarrying shaped the riverside for a century, which is why so much of the newer housing sits on reworked ground and why some gardens end at a cliff of white rock. Uphill, the chalk is overlain in places by clays and gravels that hold water for longer, so two streets a few hundred metres apart can behave quite differently after a wet week.

What we find most often in Dartford

  • Episodic damp on chalk during sustained rain, pointing at surface water rather than rising damp
  • Raised yards and drives bridging the damp proof course on terraces and interwar semis
  • High water table and made ground affecting solid floors near the riverside and marsh
  • Blocked airbricks starving the void under suspended timber floors in the older streets
  • Condensation and mould in newer riverside flats where trickle vents are kept shut and fans are undersized

Rising damp, penetrating damp or condensation?

They can look the same on a Dartford wall: a stain, a tide mark, blown plaster or mould. They behave differently. Rising damp is steady through the year and highest at the base of a wall. Penetrating damp follows the weather and sits where water has a route in, around a window, under a gutter, on the exposed side. Condensation is worst in cold weather, on the coldest surfaces, and often comes with streaming windows.

A meter alone cannot tell them apart, and salts in old plaster make every damp meter read high. The pattern, the readings at depth, the outside of the building and the room conditions together can, which is why the survey takes the time it does.

Inside and out: the Dartford survey, step by step

Most damp is decided outside the house, so that is where half the visit goes. On a typical Dartford property we check:

  • The outside walls first: pointing, render, sills, and how yellow stock brick has been repaired over the years
  • Where the ground sits against the wall, front and back, and whether a patio or flower bed has crept up
  • Rainwater: gutters, hoppers, downpipes and where they discharge, ideally after we have seen them in rain
  • Damp readings inside on a pattern, with a reference reading from a dry wall for comparison
  • Salts, tide marks and blown plaster, which say what the water has been doing for years
  • Room conditions: humidity, dew point and the coldest surfaces, for any mould
  • Timber in reach: skirtings, joist ends, sub floor and the loft hatch view of the roof

Buying or letting in Dartford

Buying in Dartford often means choosing between an older terrace near the station and a newer home by the river. They fail differently. On the terrace, a homebuyer report flagging damp usually means ground level or rainwater, and the fix is modest if it is diagnosed properly. On the new build, damp in the first year or two is often construction moisture or ventilation, and some of it may still be the developer's responsibility under warranty. Either way, an independent survey before exchange tells you which.

Letting in Dartford? Damp and mould in rented homes are a hazard under the Housing Health and Safety Rating System, and social landlords in England now have fixed timescales to investigate and act under Awaab's Law, with the government planning to extend similar duties to private renting. An independent report that separates building defects from condensation is the evidence both landlords and tenants need. See our guide to landlord damp and mould duties.

Damp survey questions from Dartford

The damp appears after heavy rain then dries out. What does that mean?

It very largely rules out rising damp, which is slow and steady and does not respond to individual weather events. Damp that tracks the rainfall is arriving from outside: surface water reaching the wall, an overflowing gutter or hopper, driving rain on an exposed elevation, or water moving quickly through chalk. That is genuinely useful evidence, so it is worth noting when it happens and what the weather was doing.

Our house is on redeveloped industrial land. Does that matter?

It can. Made ground is variable in a way natural ground is not, so drainage and services laid through it settle differently, and water can find routes that would not exist in undisturbed chalk. It does not make damp inevitable, but it does mean the external investigation matters more: ground falls, drainage runs and the condition of gullies and manholes.

We have mould in a newer flat near the river. Is that our fault?

Rarely entirely. Newer flats are airtight by design and rely on mechanical extract and trickle vents to move moisture out. If a fan moves a fraction of the air it should, or extracts into a void, the flat will grow mould whatever you do. We measure it so the report can say whether it is a building matter.

Do you cover Bean, Sutton at Hone and the Darent valley villages?

Yes, along with Wilmington, Hawley, Darenth, Longfield and the rest of the borough. The fee is the same across it.

Our cellar is damp after rain. Do we need it tanked?

Not necessarily. On chalk, water arriving in a cellar with rainfall often comes from a defective drain, a gully or surface water at one corner, and fixing that is far cheaper than tanking. The survey looks at the outside drainage before anything internal is considered.
