Damp survey in Whitstable

Whitstable is a working harbour town that became a seaside destination, and its buildings show both. Weatherboarded fishermen's cottages and net stores crowd the old streets behind the beach, Victorian terraces fill the town, Tankerton has its slopes and larger Edwardian houses, and Chestfield and Swalecliffe are largely twentieth century. Salt air, onshore wind and low ground by the sea shape the damp here more than anything else.

Whitstable homes, and how they get damp

The old town around Island Wall, Harbour Street and the alleys between them has small weatherboarded and brick cottages, often timber framed, close to the beach and below the level of the sea wall. Weatherboarding works as a rain screen when it is maintained, and fails when boards split, laps are painted shut, or ground and shingle build up against the bottom boards and the sole plate behind.

The Victorian and Edwardian terraces across the town are solid brick, many painted or rendered, with bay windows facing the weather. Salt carried on the wind collects in masonry and plaster, keeping meter readings high long after a wall has dried, and making damp look worse than it is. That makes interpreting readings much more important than taking them.

Tankerton, Chestfield and Swalecliffe include larger Edwardian and interwar houses and a great deal of post war housing. Here, damp is mostly condensation and ventilation, flat roof and extension junctions, and exposure on the seaward elevations.

Ground, water and weather in Whitstable

Whitstable sits on London Clay at the edge of the Swale estuary, with the land low behind the sea wall in the old town and Seasalter, and rising at Tankerton, where the grassy slopes above the beach are a reminder that clay cliffs move. The low streets have a high water table and are protected by sea defences. Clay drains poorly, so gardens and yards hold water against walls in winter.

Cottages below the sea wall

Many of Whitstable's oldest cottages sit below the level of the sea wall and the beach, on ground that has been raised around them over the years by shingle, paving and the build up of streets. Their ground floors are low, their walls were built without damp proof courses, and their floors were often originally brick or boards on earth.

In these cottages, a little damp at low level is a fact of life, and the aim is to manage it so that the building stays healthy: keep the base of the walls clear, let the walls and floors breathe, keep the rain off and the gutters running, and ventilate. Sealing them with waterproof plaster or a concrete floor moves the moisture somewhere less visible and less forgiving.

Higher up the town and in Tankerton, the houses are larger and drier, and the questions shift to exposure and rainwater. A survey in Whitstable starts by working out which kind of house it is.

Rising damp, penetrating damp or condensation?

They can look the same on a Whitstable wall: a stain, a tide mark, blown plaster or mould. They behave differently. Rising damp is steady through the year and highest at the base of a wall. Penetrating damp follows the weather and sits where water has a route in, around a window, under a gutter, on the exposed side. Condensation is worst in cold weather, on the coldest surfaces, and often comes with streaming windows.

A meter alone cannot tell them apart, and salts in old plaster make every damp meter read high. The pattern, the readings at depth, the outside of the building and the room conditions together can, which is why the survey takes the time it does.

Inside and out: the Whitstable survey, step by step

Most damp is decided outside the house, so that is where half the visit goes. On a typical Whitstable property we check:

  • The outside walls first: pointing, render, sills, and how weatherboarding and brick has been repaired over the years
  • Where the ground sits against the wall, front and back, and whether a patio or flower bed has crept up
  • Rainwater: gutters, hoppers, downpipes and where they discharge, ideally after we have seen them in rain
  • Damp readings inside on a pattern, with a reference reading from a dry wall for comparison
  • Salts, tide marks and blown plaster, which say what the water has been doing for years
  • Room conditions: humidity, dew point and the coldest surfaces, for any mould
  • Timber in reach: skirtings, joist ends, sub floor and the loft hatch view of the roof

What the written report contains

Damp survey questions from Whitstable

Why do meter readings stay high on our seaside walls?

Salt carried on the wind collects in masonry and plaster and attracts moisture from the air, so meters read high even on walls that are not wet from any source. Readings at depth and a look at the pattern separate salts from water.

Our weatherboarded cottage has soft boards at the bottom. Is it serious?

It can be, because the sole plate behind the boards is structural. The survey checks how far decay has gone and why: often shingle, paving or ground has built up against the boarding.

Is our house at risk from the sea?

Flood risk is a matter for the Environment Agency maps and your insurer. What we can tell you is how the building is coping with the moisture it receives and what will help.

Do you cover Seasalter, Chestfield and Swalecliffe?

Yes, and Tankerton, Herne Bay and the rest of the Canterbury district.

Our Tankerton bay window lets in water. What is the cause?

Usually the bay roof junction, leadwork or sill, which take the full force of north and north easterly weather. The survey identifies the point of entry.

How soon can you visit?

Same day reply and usually a visit within a few days.

Will you try to sell me damp proofing in Whitstable?

No. DampScan is a survey and report practice and carries out no remedial work of any kind. Where work is needed, the report specifies it so any contractor can price it, and where it is not, the report says so.
