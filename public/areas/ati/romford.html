Independent damp surveys in Romford

Romford is a market town that London grew around. The old centre by the market and the station is Victorian and Edwardian, Gidea Park is an early garden suburb, Collier Row and Rush Green are interwar, Harold Hill is one of the big post-war estates, and the streets near the station have filled with new flats. Each of those does damp differently, and a survey in Romford starts with knowing which one you are standing in.

Romford housing, and what it does when it gets wet

Close to the market and the station the houses are Victorian and Edwardian terraces with solid brick walls, many rendered in cement since and most with a rear addition. Here the findings are the solid wall ones: render that holds water against the brick, internal plaster and skirtings that bridge whatever damp proof course there is, and chimney breasts wet from a stack that was capped without ventilation. Flats above shops along South Street and the Market Place add shared roofs and parapet gutters, where the leak and the damage can be two floors apart.

Gidea Park was built as a showcase suburb before the First World War, with Arts and Crafts houses in roughcast, tile hanging and brick, and much of it is a conservation area. Roughcast painted over so many times it can no longer dry, tile hanging with failed battens and felt behind it, and leadwork on complicated roofs are what we find there, and any repair has to suit the house as well as stop the water.

Collier Row, Rush Green, Mawneys and Ardleigh Green are mostly interwar and post-war semis with cavity walls and a working course, and Harold Hill is a large estate of the late 1940s and 1950s. On these the damp is overwhelmingly drives and patios raised above the course, blocked airbricks under timber floors, and condensation in rooms where windows were replaced without trickle vents. The new flats near the station are a ventilation question almost every time.

What we are usually called out to in Romford

  • Drives and patios laid above the damp proof course on interwar semis
  • Cement render on Victorian terraces near the market trapping moisture in the brick

  • Overpainted roughcast and failing tile hanging in Gidea Park
  • Condensation and black mould in flats and in houses with sealed replacement windows
  • Leaking parapet and valley gutters on flats above shops in the town centre

Which kind of damp is it?

In a London building the same stain can have four or five different causes, and in Romford we regularly find two at once. Water rising from the ground through a wall with no working course. Rain entering through a parapet, a gutter, a cracked render or a window head. Moisture from the air condensing on a cold wall. Water from a pipe, a bath seal or a flat upstairs. Each needs a different repair and, in a shared building, often a different person to pay for it.

Our readings are taken to separate them: at depth as well as at the surface, at heights up the wall, on either side of the damp, and alongside humidity and surface temperatures. The pattern, not a single number, is what the diagnosis rests on.

How we survey a Romford property

  • Externally first: the elevations, the ground or paving against them, and every route water has to the wall
  • For flats, the parts of the building outside the demise that can be seen or reasonably reached
  • Readings that distinguish surface salts from moisture in depth, rather than a single pin meter figure
  • Dew point conditions in the affected rooms, logged and compared with the coldest surfaces
  • Ventilation measured, not assumed from the presence of a fan
  • Previous damp treatments identified, with whether they addressed the cause

Romford questions

Our 1930s semi in Collier Row has damp at the bottom of the front wall. Does it need a new damp proof course?

Rarely. Houses of that age usually still have a working course. Look at the drive or path outside: if it sits less than about two brick courses below the course, rain splashes over it and the ground bridges it. That is fixed outside, at the paving, not by injecting the wall.

We are buying in Gidea Park and the homebuyer report mentions damp. What next?

Gidea Park houses are often roughcast or tile hung, and a meter reading through those finishes on its own says little. An independent survey finds where the water is actually coming from, whether that is the render, the roof or the ground, before you exchange.

Our flat near the station has black mould on the bedroom walls. Is it damp?

Almost always it is condensation: warm moist air meeting a cold wall, often where extraction is weak or trickle vents stay shut. We measure humidity, surface temperature and the extract fans, so the report says what will make the difference.

Do you cover Harold Hill and Harold Wood?

Yes, and Rush Green, Mawneys, Ardleigh Green and Chadwell Heath, along with the rest of Havering.

Can you look at a damp proofing quote we have been given?

Yes. Send it with your request and the report will say which parts are supported by what we find and which are not. We sell no treatment ourselves.

Will you try to sell me damp proofing in Romford?

No. We carry out no remedial work, so there is nothing to sell after the survey. The report states the cause, the work that would actually fix it and an outline of what that work should reasonably cost, so you can get like for like quotes from contractors of your choice, or put it to a seller, landlord or freeholder.
